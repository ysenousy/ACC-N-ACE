aimed at preventing and controlling fires, ensuring the safety of individuals, and minimizing property damage [48]. to achieve these protection objectives, fire safety regulations are a cornerstone of national and regional build- ing codes. these regulations dictate mandatory measures concerning building design, construction, and operation. while specifics may vary between countries, common elements include design requirements for accessible escape routes, structural measures to contain fire develop- ment, and proactive measures for defensive fire protection [4953]. to meet these requirements, fire protection considerations must be integrated throughout all stages of a buildings life cycle. particu- larly in the o&m phase, ensuring continuous compliance with the fire protection objectives is crucial for both preventive and defensive fire protection. therefore, conducting regular fire safety inspections is a legal responsibility to ensure the safety of occupants. fire safety regulations mandate building operators to conduct regular inspections of fire safety-related systems and equipment, which involves physi- cally visiting the facility and systematically assessing relevant assets to verify compliance. in general, neglecting these inspections can lead to increased safety hazards [54]. a valuable tool for this purpose is a fire safety inspection checklist, aiding in the systematic evaluation of essential elements of structural, plant, organizational, and defensive fire protection in an existing building. while no standardized procedure covers all specific items, various guides and resources are available as working aids [17,21]. bim models with fse information can enhance the efficiency and efficacy of inspections. they do not just provide any existing inventory of fse information, but can also help inspectors (and even robots) navigate to all components [5558]. they can also serve a user-friendly interface to update any fse information [59]. 2.2. fire safety-related scan-to-bim building upon the importance of fire safety inspections, integrating fire safety-related information into bim models is a crucial aspect of advancing the o&m phase of a building. various sensor-based and data-driven approaches are advocated in the literature for an efficient scan-to-bim process [6064]. one notable approach by vandecasteele et al. [61] utilizes video object recognition linked with bim to enhance fire safety management, providing feedback on location estimation, fire location, evacuation guidance, and fire forecasting. moreover, researchers have specifically investigated the recognition and pose estimation of mechanical, elec- trical, and plumbing (mep) components in 3d point clouds [60,62,65]. notably, adan et al. [62] highlight the limitations of using orthoimages generated from point clouds for component detection, emphasizing the need for alternative strategies. in other studies [66,67], researchers focus on training object detection neural networks to identify fire assets like extinguishers and exit signs in images, leveraging transfer learning from the coco dataset weights. as the above review shows, despite a growing interest in automating the scan-to-bim process for mep and fse, existing literature predom- inantly focuses on on-site data acquisition techniques like interior photography or lidar scanning. however, a compelling argument exists for leveraging building documentation instead, given its ease of accessibility and inclusion of valuable semantic information. conse- quently, there is merit in exploring whether the application of 2d plan analysis, a technique successfully employed in tasks such as symbol detection in floor plans or other technical drawings [32,3443,68], could benefit scan-to-bim in this context. while hassaan et al. [45] pro- posed a digitization method for escape plans, including detection of fse symbols, key limitations require further exploration. for example, if an fse components location is indicated by an auxiliary line, it cannot be inferred by existing methods. furthermore, additional information on the escape plans, e.g., building story numbers, are not considered. thus, this study investigates the subject further, addressing the identified research gaps. 2.3. escape plans escape plans (see example in fig. 1) play a crucial role in inform- ing individuals within a building about available escape routes, thus ensuring the safety of occupants in the event of a fire. in accordance with the guidelines set by iso 23601 [44], these plans are specifically designed for public areas and workplaces. while they may not delve into the intricacies of detailed technical drawings meant for specialized professionals, they provide clear and concise information about escape routes, emergency exits, and the locations of fire safety equipment. to maintain a clear and consistent representation of equipment in plans, international and national requirements adhere to standards for safety colors and signs, as outlined in iso 7010 [69]. thus, if first aid and fire-fighting facilities are available in the building, the plan should utilize safety signs that comply with iso 7010. fig. 1 showcases the application of standardized symbols and color codes for clarity. this standardized representation proves advantageous for deep learning-based symbol detection techniques, as it facilitates a low generalization error. also, since escape plans are required by law to be readily available and displayed in strategic locations in buildings, they are a convenient source of fse information for this study. automationinconstruction162(2024)1053822 p. schnfelder et al. fig. 1. example escape plan. 2.4. research gaps and formulation of research problems the preceding sections highlight the significance of integrating fire safety information into bim models, particularly focusing on the utilization of escape plans as a potential data source. however, several research gaps and challenges have emerged. first, while there is a trend towards automating scan-to-bim for fire safety and mep components, current methods rely on on-site data gathering, such as photography or lidar scanning. this raises questions about the viability of using alternative sources like escape plans, which may offer valuable seman- tic information and easier accessibility. second, existing digitization methods for escape plans have limitations. for instance, they struggle with auxiliary lines indicating fse locations and overlook additional information like building story numbers. to address these issues, we pose the following research questions:  how can escape plans be used to automatically integrate fire safety data into bim models?  what are the limitations of current escape plan digitization meth- ods, and how can they be improved for accurate fse extraction?  how can advanced techniques like keypoint-based object de- tection enhance fse extraction from escape plans, considering standardized symbols and color codes? addressing these research problems aims to contribute to the devel- opment of more efficient methods for enriching bim models with fire safety-related information, thereby enhancing building management practices. 3. methodology an overview of the proposed process to incorporate fse information into bim models is depicted in fig. 2. first, an escape plan is captured by the user (a) and pre-processed for further analysis (b). details on these steps are provided in section 3.1 and section 3.2, respectively. next, the required information is extracted from the plan. first, sec- tion 3.3 introduces a keypoint-based deep learning symbol detection network for localizing the fse on the plan (c). then, section 3.4 details how the text information on the plan is utilized to infer the depicted building floor (e). to obtain the fse components model coordinates, the captured image is registered with the geometric model of the building floor (d), and the corresponding coordinate transformation is applied to the pixel coordinates of the located symbol positions (f). finally, the enrichment of the bim model with the fse components is capturing the proposed method prioritizes simplicity in application, aiming to provide a user-friendly solution. therefore, it does not rely on meticulously curated high-quality scans of escape plans. instead, a basic smartphone image of the wall-mounted escape plans is suffi- cient, as long as the resolution enables precise positioning of fse. the assumption is made that each input image captures the entire wall- mounted escape plan without significant rotation, distortion, or lighting reflections. 3.2. region cropping following the capture of escape plans, the next step is to prepare the image for fse symbol detection. in this regard, it is necessary to crop the image to the actual floor plan including the fse locations, since the escape plan also includes additional text and a legend for all symbols, which could result in false positives during equipment localization. therefore, the image undergoes a deep learning-based region of inter- est (roi) cropping step, focusing on the actual floor plan. in this study, the roi is defined as the area enclosed by the smallest axis-aligned rectangular bounding box of the escape plan, which encompasses the entirety of the floor plan geometry. typically, this excludes significant portions of the legend, plan header, and other supplementary text elements. an example roi, derived from the example escape plan in fig. 1, is shown in figure fig. 3. previous studies have already shown the successful application of yolo models for engineering drawing view separation problems [70,71]. in this study, a yolov81 object detection model is employed to automate the roi cropping step. the model is trained to identify and delineate the floor plan regions on escape plans, enabling the extraction of these regions from the image for further processing. 3.3. fse symbol detection the next crucial task is the precise localization of fse symbols within the escape plan. certain drawing characteristics suggest the use of keypoint-based detection methods, as illustrated in fig. 4. in some instances, the symbols location on the plan aligns with the corresponding physical fse components location in the building, as demonstrated in fig. 4(a). however, there are cases where the symbol icon is not directly placed in the drawing corresponding to the physical component location. instead, it may be situated nearby, with an aux- iliary line indicating the exact location (true location) [45], as shown in fig. 4(b). when multiple fse components share the same location, they are grouped by an encapsulating box, and their position is denoted by a single shared auxiliary line (cf. fig. 4(c)). finally, some symbols represent multiple instances of the component and, therefore, indicate additional true locations as seen in fig. 4(d). given these nuances, when a mismatch occurs between the fse components actual location and the symbol icon, an additional point must be detected, which calls for a keypoint-based detection approach rather than bounding box-based detection. moreover, the manual cap- turing of the plans imposes slight distortions on the drawing, leading to skewed symbol icons, which cannot be mimicked with axis-aligned bounding boxes. thus, bounding box-based labeling does not suffice to specify the symbol location in all cases. instead, we propose the use of a keypoint-based annotation scheme, a methodology that has shown promising results in similar studies [43]. detailed descriptions of the developed symbol detection method are provided in the following sections. first, section 3.3.1 specifies the annotation scheme utilized to create training data for the keypoint detection network based on the acquired real escape plan imagery 1 https://github.com/ultralytics/ultralytics we draw inspiration from the human pose estimation task to establish a labeling strategy, as illustrated in fig. 5. for each symbol, a skeleton of 5 keypoints is created. keypoints 14 represent the four corners of the symbol icon, while keypoint 5 defines the position indicated by the position marker on the escape plan. special attention is given to two cases:  when an fse symbol has two position markers, denoting two components of the same kind at different locations, two skeleton instances are created. keypoints 14 of both instances share the same locations in the image, ensuring the capture of all instances, even if only one symbol is depicted.  if no position marker is present for an fse symbol, the position of the component is assumed to be the closest wall. in such cases, keypoint 5 is set accordingly to represent this position. this approach addresses potential ambiguities and ensures the compre- hensive capture of all instances of fse symbols. in instances where no position marker is available, the proposed method provides a reliable approximation of the components location, similar to what human perception might yield in such scenarios. 3.3.2. training data enhancement, this enrichment sets the stage for various use cases, including fire safety inspection. 5. discussion 5.1. implications this study presents a novel method with the potential to streamline building modeling. the innovation of the study is two-fold. first, it introduces a semi- automatic bim model enrichment method that relies on escape plans as the primary data source. notably, the applications: first, while the current approach extracts positional information solely from escape plans, future research could leverage fire protection concept documents to infuse additional semantic details into the bim model. this would provide a more comprehensive understanding of fire safety equipment components beyond their spatial locations. second, examining the campus dataset reveals a natural imbalance in class support, primarily caused by real-world building conditions (cf. table 1). this imbalance may lead to false negatives in learning-based symbol detection methods, as observed in fig. 9(b). future studies focusing on escape plans could address this by incorporating modi- fications to the deep learning models loss function or implementing advanced data sampling techniques. notably, deliberate sampling of training escape plans may not fully rectify this imbalance, given its reflection within each escape plan, where certain symbols are more prevalent than others. third, automating the registration process between escape plans and the ifc building model remains an open challenge. subsequent research efforts should concentrate on this aspect of the pipeline, exploring techniques such as deep learning-based segmentation methods to ex- tract floor outlines. additionally, identifying key building components like doors could serve as anchor points for registration, enhancing the automation of this critical step. fourth, future studies could delve into the extraction of informa- tion concerning fire safety equipment that is not explicitly outlined in escape plans, with the aim of integrating these components into bim models. key elements such as sprinkler systems, fire doors, and smoke evacuators undoubtedly play a role in enhancing fire safety by extending available safe evacuation times. therefore, their inclusion in bim models holds significance for accurate emergency response simulations and strategies [83]. finally, a novel suggestion for future exploration involves an inter- disciplinary study where the proposed method is employed to create an inventory and derive a checklist for equipment checking. subsequently, the building model and checklist could be utilized for inspection route planning, potentially leading to an autonomous inspection process executed by robots. additionally, the inspection results could be used to further enrich the bim model with semantic information about the fse, e.g., the fire extinguishers expiry dates, manufacturers, and types (co2, foam). such an integrated approach holds the promise of showcasing the full spectrum of automation potentials in safety inspections, providing a robust framework for efficient and accurate assessments. 6. conclusions in conclusion, this study presents a novel methodology for enriching existing bim models with fse components extracted from escape plans. leveraging advanced deep learning techniques, the proposed approach demonstrates promising outcomes in the automation of this process, offering several insights and avenues for further research. the proposed method has potential to facilitate the enrichment of bim models with fse components, addressing widely unexplored aspects of retrospective building modeling, e.g., small building compo- nents modeling. despite its achievements, the proposed methodology has limitations that should be considered. first, it primarily focuses on inferring precise positions of fse components, neglecting semantic data aspects that could enhance comprehensive inspections. second, the reliance on escape plans assumes their accuracy in representing as-designed and as-is locations, potentially impacting the usefulness of the enriched 3d model for visualization. additionally, the manual user input required for calculating the coordinate transformation from photograph to model view remains a notable aspect that demands further exploration for automation. looking ahead, future research endeavors should aim to extract semantic information from fire protection concept documents, address class imbalances in symbol detection, automate the registration process, and explore an integrated approach for creating inventories, checklists, and autonomous inspection routes using the proposed method. these extensions could unlock additional layers of insight and functionality, advancing the potential
