attention from the scientific community [1,2], in particular from the scientific community working in artificial intelligence (ai) [3,4]. as a result of the gradual increase in popularity of these ai approaches propose to use legal knowledge repre- sentations in vectorized formats compatible with nlp post-analyses with respect to the in-force norms [18]. with the popularity of large language models (llms), it is expected that, in the near future, these approaches as well as the whole research in nlp for ai and law will be refined and extended in light of the potential offered by llms; preliminary investigations in that direction can be found in [1922]. nevertheless, since ml is based on statistical reasoning, it is difficult for it to work around specific and exact values, a requirement common in tasks such as scrutinizing com- pliance in compliance filing documents. this is, in particular, the case when compliance checking requires inferences on the explicitly asserted values. thus, due to mls statistical reasoning approach in handling represented legal knowledge, the specific or exact values which are required for inferential reasoning are often either non-existent in the representa- tion or are not clearly captured. this makes it difficult to make inferences from the legal information 2024, 15, 759. https://doi.org/10.3390/info15120759 https://www.mdpi.com/journal/information information 2024, 15, 759 2 of 27 knowledge represented to ascertain whether the legal rule in question has either been complied with or violated. another limitation of the vectorized representations typically used in ml is that their results cannot be fully explained. this is because, once they undergo training based on statistical reasoning, they often behave like a black box, lacking the ability to elucidate their decision-making process. consequently, it becomes challenging, and at times even impossible, to clarify the reason why the ml system took certain decisions. to address these limitations, symbolic representations have been proposed, even though they require more manual efforts, as, in contemporary approaches, symbols and rules are usually written and debugged by hand [23,24]. symbols are suitable for dealing with specific and exact values. in addition, they correspond to human-understandable concepts, while rules represent how we reason with them; thus, the chain of logical derivations on symbols could provide intelligible explanations of ai decision making. in the ai and law domain, another indirect advantage that symbolic reasoning has over ml is that the former facilitates the identification of gaps within the law because of their precise and structured representation. simply put, while ml reasons by statistical similarity and relevance, symbolic approaches adopt more fine-grained representations that work only if all pieces of the puzzle are present and placed in the right place. this method aids in detecting discrepancies and gaps in legal reasoning, as it explicitly represents legal concepts and their relationships as well as inherent logical inferences. ml is not adept at identifying gaps within the law since it lacks the understanding of legal concepts and it only relies on identifying recurring patterns in data. ml is effective for tasks like document classification but struggles with the nuanced nature of legal language, which is crucial in legal reasoning. in contrast, because of the explicit representation of symbolic formalization, human lawyers are able to systematically identify and address gaps within the law. an example of this advantage will be shown in section 2.1 below: the formalization of the norms from our case study revealed to us a lacuna in the regulations, which was not so easy to identify at first glance. to mitigate the fact that symbolic representations and rules require more manual ef- it has been proposed to use standardized formats, namely w3c standards forts, (https://www.w3.org/2001/sw/wiki/main_page, accessed 17 november 2024) such as rdf, sparql, owl, and shacl, which have already been successfully employed for semantic web emerge in the near future, combining the statistical reasoning characteristic of machine learning with the logical reasoning that underpins knowledge graphs. acc frameworks developed with semantic web technologies have rdf graphs at their core [27]. examples are [2831], etc., which all use rdf graphs or data as the basis for their acc frameworks, although they propose different approaches to reasoning with the rdf graphs. this paper contributes to research on semantic web technology approaches for acc, particularly in conducting aggregate and temporal reasoning on rdf graphs or data. we illustrate our approach using a case study from ghanas upstream petroleum industry, with a specific focus on local content and participation. unlike the majority of solutions proposed for automated reasoning via rdf, our representations will be based on the shapes and constraints language (shacl) (https: //www.w3.org/tr/shacl, accessed on 17 november 2024), specifically, the new proposed extension of shacl-sparql rules (https://www.w3.org/tr/shacl-af/#sparqlrule, accessed on 17 november 2024). as is well known, shacl was not proposed for reasoning with rdf data, but rather for validating rdf data. the main w3c standard for reasoning on rdf data is instead web information 2024, 15, 759 3 of 27 ontology language (owl) (https://www.w3.org/owl, accessed on 17 november 2024), and a lot of research grounded on owl has been carried out in the last decades in ai and law and beyond (see section 4 below). owl vocabulary allows for the specification of owl axioms that describe inferences; in particular, the main inference mechanisms in owl are the owl restrictions, which permit us to restrict the meaning of an ontological class by imposing all instances of that class to feature certain properties. in formal terms, an owl restriction is an anonymous ontological class, defined by only describing its properties; then, by stipulating that other named ontological classes are subclasses of that owl restriction, the properties defined on the latter are inferred for all instances of the named class via is-a inheritance [32,33]. however, it is well known that owl is not expressive enough for representing infer- ence rules needed in practical case studies for legal reasoning, such as the one addressed in this paper. for this reason, as will be discussed in section 4 below, other rule-based languages, more expressive than owl, have been used to complement or even to replace owl, thus making the role of the w3c standard somehow vacuous. an example of these rule-based languages is the aforementioned shacl-sparql rules, although they have not yet been released as part of an official w3c recommendation, as they remain at the stage of a w3c working group note. section 4 below explains why we chose shacl-sparql rules as the formal language to implement our solution. on the other hand, for building the legal ontology for the use case, on which the shacl-sparql rules for acc are executed, we employed a mixed-methods approach to ontology engineering [34,35]. specifically, we utilized the melon (methodology for build- ing legal ontology) methodology [11] alongside the o2 validation framework from [36], which combines structural, usability, and functional dimensions for ontology assessment. our approach adapts melon to incorporate shacl-sparql rules that enable automated reasoning as well as validation for compliance checking within the chosen domain. the rest of this paper is organized as follows: section 2 discusses the case study used in this paper to exemplify the need for formalizing compliance checking on rdf data via shacl-sparql instead of owl, while section 3 describes the methodology we employed in developing and norms from the existing regulations in ghanas upstream petroleum industry for which the next sections will show that the expressivity offered by owl does not suffice to represent their meaning. the upstream petroleum industry is one sector that has contributed quite keenly to ghanas economy, and it is expected to contribute even further. the sector is a multibillion- dollar sector and, as a result, contributes quite significantly to ghanas gdp. for example, between 2013 and 2021, 4.5% of ghanas gdp was attributed to the oil and gas sector (https:// www.businessghana.com/site/news/business/278790/oil-and-gas-contributes-4-5-per-cent- to-ghana-s-gdp-in-the-last-8-years, accessed on 17 november 2024). additionally, it has been estimated that the oil and gas industry will contribute ap- proximately ghs 15.94 billion (around 1.3 billion u.s. dollars (the dollar equivalent of information 2024, 15, 759 4 of 27 ghs 15.94 billion was calculated based on the exchange rate as at 30 november 2023)) to ghanas gross domestic product in 2024 [37]. given the upstream petroleum industrys economic significance, it is not surprising that it is one of the most heavily regulated sectors in ghana. as of the last count, there are about 19 pieces of legislation governing the sector, ranging from the constitution, which is the supreme law of ghana, to guidelines and a model petroleum agreement (https://petrocom.gov.gh/lawsandregulations/, accessed on 17 november 2024). several scholars in the domain, e.g., ref. [38], have suggested that any oil and gas producing country, in order to maximize its economic benefit from its petroleum resources, must adopt local content and participation, which is, in essence, the use and involvement of indigenous materials and personnel in the said industry. in order to enhance local content and participation, ghana has promulgated the petroleum (local content and local participation) regulations 2013 (l.i 2204 (https:// petrocom.gov.gh/wp-content/uploads/2022/08/13-local-content-and-local-participation- regulations-l_i-2204.pdf, accessed on 17 november 2024)) hereinafter referred to as li 2204 (li 2204 has been recently amended by the petroleum (local content and local participation) (amendment) regulations, 2021 (l.i. 2435) https://www.petrocom.gov.gh/l&c_folder/pet_ register/laws/li%202435.pdf, accessed on 17 november 2024). this piece of legislation regulates the local content and participation of ghanas upstream petroleum industry. specifically, according to regulation 49 of li 2204, local content refers to the percentage or quantum of materials, personnel, financing, goods, and services generated by indigenous companies for the upstream petroleum value chain and can be monetarily quantified (regulation 49 of petroleum (local content and local participation) regulations, 2013 (l.i 2204)). therefore, locally produced goods, services that are part of the value chain of the ghanaian upstream petroleum industry, and anything else produced locally which is of monetary value can be classified as local content. on the other hand, local participation has not been defined by the li 2204. however, a definition may be found on the website of the petroleum commission; according to the said website, local participation has to do with the level of ghanaian ownership in the upstream petroleum industry (https://www.petrocom.gov.gh/local-content, accessed on 17 november 2024). therefore, the extent to which ghanaians own companies, facilities, properties, and other assets involved in the upstream petroleum industry is what is referred to as local participation. this is to say that the primary goal of l.i 2204 is to promote the involvement of ghanaian citizens and the utilization of local resources within the upstream oil and gas sector. in essence, this legislation seeks to restrict foreign companies from importing their personnel and materials. conversationally, these companies are granted permission to extract the nations oil and gas, provided they generate employment opportunities and contribute to other economic advantages for the local population. to this end, violations of the provisions of li 2204 and its amendment will attract sanctions (regulation 46 of the petroleum (local content and local participation) regulations, 2013 (l.i 2204)). the petroleum commission, which is the regulatory authority for ghanas upstream industry, is responsible for ensuring that participants in the industry abide by li 2204 and its amendment. one of the ways specified by li 2204 for the petroleum commission to check whether the participants of the industry are complying with the regulations is through the submission of compliance filing documents such as the local content plan, the local content performance report, etc. the commission will then ascertain from the submitted compliance filing documents whether the participants have either complied or violated the regulations. the compliance filing documents are currently accessible on the petroleum commis- sions website in both excel, word, and pdf formats. industry participants download these files and manually complete them, submitting the filled documents to the petroleum commission, where they undergo manual verification. as part of his ph.d. research, the first author of this paper is now working towards the development of a web applica- information 2024, 15, 759 5 of 27 tion to replace these excel, word, and pdf files. the envisioned application will enable industry participants to complete these documents online while allowing for automated or semi-automated compliance checks of the regulatory requirements. our framework formalizes five major regulations and three sections of the sched- ules in li 2204 using the methodology discussed in section 3. due to the impracticality of exhaustively discussing all these provisions in this paper, we selected representative examples, which we will present in the next subsection. most of the other regulations can be formalized in owl or the other formal languages for legal reasoning discussed in section 4 below. the provisions presented in the next subsection, on the contrary, cannot be formalized by any of them as only shacl-sparql provides the required expressivity. 2.1. key provisions motivating the use of shacl-sparql for temporal and aggregate reasoning as stated supra, participants in ghanas oil and gas industry are required to submit compliance filing documents to the petroleum commission for it to check whether or not the participants are complying with the regulations. regulation 7(2)(b) of li 2204 states to the effect that participants (contractors, subcontractors, licensees, and other allied entities) must submit an annual local content plan (lcp) to provide evidence of their compliance with li 2204. the lcp contains several sub-plans, for the purposes of this research, such as the employment and training sub-plan requiring the companies to specify details of their employees. each participant must submit its lcp every year; the petroleum commission establishes a window of submission that is usually opened on the first of january of the year until the last day of february. participants are obliged to submit the lcp only within the established window of submission. thus, a company that fails to submit its local content plan commits an offense and is liable to pay to the petroleum commission a penalty of five percent of the value of the proceeds obtained from the petroleum activity but not exceeding the cedi equivalent of usd five million (regulation 46 (7) (b) of the petroleum (local content and local participation) regulations, 2013 (l.i 2204)). the first schedule of li 2204 provides the minimum percentages of ghanaian employ- ees that the participants should employ at every level of employment depending on the number of years the company has been operational. for instance, it states to the effect inter alia that (section 3 (a), (b), and (c) of part 1 of the first schedule of petroleum (local content and local participation) regulations, 2013 (l.i 2204).): (1) a. where the participant has been operational in ghana for less than five years, then it must ensure that 30% of its management staff are ghanaians. b. where the participant has been operational in ghana for five years but less than ten years, then it must ensure that 50% to 60% of its management staff are ghanaians. c. where the participant has been operational in ghana for ten or more years, then it must ensure that 70% to 80% of its management staff are ghanaians. consequently, if a company does not employ the requisite percentages of ghanaians at any of the employment levels, that company commits an offense and is liable to pay to the petroleum commission an administrative penalty of two hundred thousand penalty units at best, and at worst, where the contravention persists, that companys name may be expunged from the petroleum register (regulation 46 (5) (b)(j) (k) (l) of the petroleum (local content and local participation) regulations, 2013 (l.i 2204)). in order to provide evidence of compliance with respect to ((1).ac), the annual local content plan, which contains the employment and training sub-plan that a participant must submit, requires the specification of all the details about the participants employment, including employees details, employment start dates, etc. from this information, it is clear that owl inferences do not suffice to check com- pliance of ((1).ac), precisely for the reasons explained in the introduction: owl lacks support for aggregate and temporal operators. the former are needed to count how many information 2024, 15, 759 6 of 27 employees, either ghanaian or non-ghanaian, the participant hired as management staff. the latter are needed to check for how long the participant has been operational in ghana. on the other hand, as already mentioned in the introduction, when trying to formalize the intended meaning of ((1).ac), we identified a lacuna in li 2204. the participants status of being operational in ghana is not a single conceptual entity that features a temporal extension, i.e., a start and and end date, as are the management staff roles. nevertheless, li 2204 does not specify how long the employees must be employed as management staff in relation to the window of submission for the annual lcp. thus, a mischievous participant may decide to promote some of its ghanaian employees on the day before the window commences and demote them the day after, just to formally comply with ((1).ac). even worse, the participant could hire new fake employees, i.e., actors, as management staff and sack them immediately after submitting the lcp. in both cases, the employees/actors that agreed to participate in the mischievous action may be bribed or rewarded with some other benefits. here, it is clear that even though the mischievous participant has technically complied with li 2204, the purpose for which li 2204 came into force, as explained above, would not be achieved. thus, as far as the employment of ghanaians is concerned, this blatant lacuna affords participants the avenue to manipulate li 2204 to their own mischievous and parochial ends. to remedy this lacuna, we recommend that the parliament of ghana consider amending li 2204 to include, for instance, an at least six-month threshold of employment/promotion in relation to the start date of the window of submission for the annual lcp. in other words, it is our suggestion that the ghanaian employees who may be counted in determining the percentages of management staff for a particular year may be those who were employed as such for at least six months before the start date of the window of submission for that year. in our implementation below, we will include this additional requirement to prove our point that symbolic representation can help in the identification and resolution of lacunae in legislation, although it is not currently enshrined in li 2204. to emphasize, this is a recommendation to the parliament of ghana to amend li 2204 to deal with the gap identified from our formalization since it is the arm of government clothed with the authority to do so. it should be also noted that we only used the six- month threshold as an example to demonstrate our point for the purpose of this paper. the parliament of ghana can decide on any time limit for the employment threshold if they so desire. flowing from this, it is therefore reasonable to make the assertion that not only does symbolic representation help to efficiently achieve acc, but it also has the capacity to aid law makers (such as the parliament of ghana in this case) in their roles of effectively promulgating new laws and refining existing ones. 3. adapting the melon methodology to incorporate shacl-sparql rules as pointed out in the previous paragraph, we built a legal ontology enriched with shacl-sparql rules to represent the provisions discussed in section 2. we used a mixed-methods approach [3436] in developing our ontology, which currently includes the following:      221 classes; 247 properties; 410 individuals; 26 shacl shapes; 48 shacl-sparql rules for acc. while the next sections focus on the subset of acc rules that require the expressivity of shacl-sparql, this section provides an overview of the approach that we used to develop the entire ontology. the starting point of our approach was the melon methodology [11], a well-known methodology used for building legal ontologies. melon reflects best practices in ontology engineering, as it is pivotal in achieving holistic and effective outcomes, particularly for the legal domain. information 2024, 15, 759 7 of 27 3.1. the melon methodology the melon methodology (methodology for building legal ontology) (the melon methodology for building legal ontologies by prof. monica palmirani is described in [11]) was created by prof. monica palmirani in 2015. it is an interdisciplinary approach for building legal ontologies. melon addresses the unique challenges of developing ontologies in the legal domain, which is known for its complex terminology as well as intricate and interrelated concepts. by providing a structured framework, melon enables experts to navigate these challenges effectively. it has been foundational in creating legal ontologies like pronto [11]. the melon methodology is made up of ten steps as follows:           step 1: provide a comprehensive description of the goal or purpose for which the legal ontology has been developed, including specific research questions or objectives and relevant use cases. step 2: define key indicators or parameters for evaluating the ontology, ensuring they align with the goals established in step 1 and cover aspects like accuracy, completeness, consistency, and usability. step 3: investigate existing ontologies that can be integrated or linked to the ontology being developed, evaluating their applicability to save time and enhance consistency and interoperability. step 4: create a comprehensive glossary of relevant terminologies sourced from various legal documents, including definitions and links to the normative sources. step 5: develop concept tables for the ontology, categorizing elements into classes, object properties, datatype properties, and owl restrictions. step 6: utilize tools like protg (https://protege.stanford.edu, accessed 17 november 2024) or yed (https://www.yworks.com/products/yed, accessed 17 november 2024) for the development of the ontology. step 7: test the ontology continuously during development. step 8: refine the ontology based on the testing phase, addressing identified errors and expanding its scope to include all necessary elements. step 9: evaluate the ontology against the parameters established in step 2 to ensure it meets the predefined criteria and standards. step 10: publish the ontology, making it publicly accessible, integrating it with other existing ontologies, and documenting it comprehensively. the melon methodology, like most other methodologies, was primarily designed for owl. however, since our approach significantly incorporates shacl-sparql rules in the ontology engineering process, not all ten steps were followed to the letter. for instance, we did not use protg because it does not support shacl-sparql. rather, we developed our own tools to execute the rules; some code from these tools is available in the github repository associated with this paper. the forthcoming phd dissertation by the first author of this paper, currently being written, will cover all ten steps in detail. some of these details are included here as well (e.g., step 1 is partially covered in section 2 above); however, most of these details are far beyond the scope of the present paper and are therefore omitted. the next subsection will just provide some additional information about step 2, specifically detailing the approaches that we used to build and evaluate our ontology. 3.2. melon, step 2 the evaluation of ontologies has been a fundamental part of ontology development since the early 1990s, as discussed in [39]. despite ongoing advancements, the literature on ontology evaluation remains fragmented and lacks a unified approach. various evaluation methods have emerged, often focusing on specific issues or multiple quality criteria simul- taneously, which has left the literature unclear on core challenges and best practices. most information 2024, 15, 759 8 of 27 evaluation efforts prioritize functionality and performance over examining the structural and compositional aspects of ontologies [36]. several evaluation methods, such as ontometric [40], ontoclean [41], and o2 [36], are used for ontology assessment. among these, o2 was chosen as the primary evaluation framework for this work, as it organizes evaluation indicators into three core dimensions: structural, usability, and functional. this framework guided the evaluation process for the legal ontology, establishing a systematic foundation and clarifying the principles underlying the assessment criteria, thereby promoting transparency in the methodology. 3.2.1. structural dimension the structural dimension of an ontology focuses on its graph representation, captur- ing both topological and logical aspects independently of specific metrics. topological properties refer to the structure of the ontologys graph, such as depth, breadth, connectiv- ity, and density, which graph-based metrics quantify to reveal its complexity, modularity, and navigability. logical properties, in contrast, deal with the ontologys formal seman- tics, ensuring consistency, inference potential, and compliance with established standards. while metrics offer quantitative insights, structural dimensions are intrinsic to the ontology, shaping its design, organization, and usability regardless of the metrics used [36]. hierarchies of classes, organized through a superclasssubclass (is-a) relationship, form the backbone of ontologies structure [4245]. to construct these hierarchies, two main methods are typically employed: the top-down approach, which starts from gen- eral concepts and refines them into specific subclasses, ensuring logical consistency and comprehensive domain understanding [46]; and the bottom-up approach, which begins with specific concepts and generalizes them into broader classes, particularly useful for well-defined, instance-rich domains [47]. a combined approach was used to develop our ontologys class hierarchy. beginning with top-down phases to establish general concepts as root nodes, then progressing to bottom-up phases to identify specific instances and generalize them into subclasses, this hybrid strategy iteratively alternated between both ap- proaches. this integration created a balanced, comprehensive class hierarchy that captures domain complexity accurately and coherently [48]. furthermore, the ontology adheres to the design patterns of the imported ontologies, among which is tvc, which we used to represent ontological entities with a temporal dimension. given its relevance to the research presented here, tvc will be explained in detail below in section 5. addition- ally, we developed tools to ensure the ontology remained logically consistent, as well as semantically and syntactically accurate, throughout its development. 3.2.2. usability dimension the usability dimensions of an evaluation parameter are heavily influenced by the level of annotation and documentation provided. a well-annotated ontology makes it easier for users to understand the properties, classes, and relationships within the ontology, as well as how they relate to real-world concepts. without proper annotation, users may struggle to recognize the intended meaning and purpose of the ontologys components [36]. in light of this, we annotated almost all the properties, classes, and individuals in our ontology through the rdf annotation property rdfs:comment. these comments defined and, in some cases, explained the said classes, properties, and individuals, thus providing proper documentation to understand the legal ontology. this paper and the forthcoming phd dissertation by the first author provide further in-depth explanations of the rdf resources in the ontology and the shacl-sparql rules for acc. 3.2.3. functional dimension the functional dimension of an ontology pertains to its purpose and the roles of its components, influenced by the context of its creation and use. in general, ontologies focus on knowledge sharing and emphasize terminological agreement; however, ontologies information 2024, 15, 759 9 of 27 designed for automation and decision support, such as the one we developed, prioritize reasoning, rule encoding, and integration capabilities [36]. in light of this, we employed a task-based approach to assess the legal ontologys functional dimension. the task-based approach is application-oriented, measuring how well an ontology enhances the performance of a designated task [34]. unlike structural eval- uations that examine conceptual hierarchy and adherence to best practices, this approach emphasizes practical, outcome-driven assessments of task support, adaptability, and tool compatibility. task-based evaluations also analyze task speed and identify inconsistencies by observing performance [34]. for instance, [49] applied this approach to evaluate an ontologys utility by measuring user effort through a complexity-based cost model. a common approach to task-based evaluation, which we employed in our work, involves listing, in advance, the competency questions (cqs) that the system is expected to answer correctly [48]. the next subsubsection lists the cqs that we postulated a priori with respect to the norms from li 2204 considered in this paper. 3.2.4. competence questions (cqs) cqs are essential for defining the requirements of ontologies and are crucial to on- tology development [48,5052]. they represent inquiries that the ontology should be able to answer, determining what is relevant for the ontology. cqs help outline its scope and provide a method for evaluation, supporting both the specification and assessment of an ontology [53]. in the specification phase, cqs assist in modeling the domain by highlighting relevant concepts and their relationships. during evaluation, they help identify flaws in domain modeling, contributing to the ontologys overall quality [46]. the aim of cqs is to ensure that ontologies accurately reflect the real world [53]. below are three examples of the cqs we defined for the ontology in step 2, i.e., before designing and implementing the ontology. cq3 relates to one of the provisions in (1).    cq1: did the contractor, subcontractor, licensee, or allied entity submit its annual local content plan? if it did, was the submission within the window of submission stipulated by the petroleum commission? [54] points to the framework presented in [55] as an example of full legal reasoning for gdpr compliance. ref. [55] em- ploys the spindle reasoner [56] to perform the necessary (defeasible) inferences. however, as recently documented in [2], the spindle reasoner has significant limitations in usability and scalability, which is instead the main focus of [54]. ref. [55] is not the only approach that addresses owls lack of expressiveness. several others have been proposed, all utilizing separate knowledge bases with rules encoded in alternative formats to represent norms that owl cannot express adequately. some of these alternative formats are: swrl (https://www.w3.org/submission/swrl, accessed 17 november 2024) [57], lkif-rules [58], ruleml [59], and legalruleml (https://www. oasis-open.org/committees/legalruleml, accessed 17 november 2024), which is the one used in [55]. however, in our view shacl-sparql is best suited for compliance checking in comparison to other rule- or ontology-based approaches such as swrl or legalruleml. shacl-sparql subsumes swrl, which itself was subsumed under the oasis stan- dard of ruleml [59]; it is the successor to the sparql inferencing notation (spin) (https://spinrdf.org, accessed 17 november 2024). legalruleml is a specialization and augmentation of ruleml; it aims at representing and interchanging legal norms. legal- ruleml is not itself designed for compliance checking, as it does not prescribe any formal semantics but requires transformations from legalruleml into a machine-readable reason- ing language, which is then provided to a defeasible reasoning engine, e.g., the spindle reasoner [55,56]. in light of this, ref. [60] made a preliminary proposal to extend the legalruleml meta model [61] and to represent normative rules via sparql queries. ref. [60] is, to our knowledge, the first proposal that models acc by employing w3c standards only. the so- lution presented in this paper is therefore rather close to [60]s; however, while [60] only uses sparql, our formalization will use shacl-sparql. similarly to [54,62,63], we distinguish compliant and non-compliant individuals by introducing, respectively, special owl subclasses and special shacl shapes. contrary to [54], however, refs. [62,63] can model defeasible inferences. for instance, the owl ontology in [62] includes two classes supplier and vehicle. the individuals in supplier are obliged to communicate their contractual conditions to their consumers (r1), while vehicles cannot drive over 90 km/h (r2). to implement r1 and r2, supplier and vehicle, respectively, include a boolean data-type property hascommunicatedconditions and a float data-type property hasdrivingspeed. then, two subclasses supplierr1compliant and vehicler2compliant are defined, the former in- cluding individuals in supplier for which hascommunicatedconditions is true, the latter including individuals in vehicle for which hasdrivingspeed is lower than 90. compliance checking is then enforced by simply applying owl2 subsumption. in other words, owl is-a inferences will populate supplierr1compliant and vehicler2compliant with only the individuals that comply with the two norms. in this setting, exceptions may be added by defining complement subclasses via the owl2 tags at disposal, e.g., owl:disjointwith. these subclasses will define the subsets of individuals that violate the norms; thus, by imposing the set of the individuals that comply with a norm as owl:disjointwith on the one that violates it, the correct inferences are achieved. information 2024, 15, 759 11 of 27 the solution in [63] is very close to the one of [62], the crucial difference being that [63] uses shacl shapes in place of owl2 subclasses/restrictions to validate the values of the relevant attributes, e.g., hascommunicatedconditions and hasdrivingspeed, in the example above. in addition, shacl triple rules are used to compute the values of these attributes. these rules collect partial data from the rdf triples, and so they facilitate the representation of the norms by decoupling it in multiple sequential steps. once the shacl triple rules have computed the values of the attributes, the shacl shapes are executed in order to validate these values. individuals with invalid attribute values are labeled as non- compliant. finally, defeasibility is modeled via negation-as-failure: whenever exceptions hold, corresponding shacl triple rules defeat other ones, so that the inferences associated with the latter are blocked. among the approaches mentioned above, only those that support sparql are ex- pressive enough to represent the norms in ((1).ac). sparql is the single mentioned formal language that supports aggregate and temporal operators. however, none of the approaches seen above discuss aggregations and temporal management, despite their crucial role in compliance checking and accountability, while this is what this paper will specifically address, as mentioned in the introduction. shacl-sparql rules have been proposed to extend shacl with inference capabili- ties, aiming towards the definition of a single unified w3c standard for both validation and inference [9,64,65]. as the name suggests, shacl-sparql extends shacl with some functionali- ties of sparql. shacl-sparql rules, in particular, are sparql queries in the form construct-where, which makes it possible to add additional triples inferred from the ones already asserted in the knowledge graph. therefore, shacl-sparql rules behave as a standard production rules system: for all rdf triples matching the where clause, the construct clause builds further triples and adds them to the knowledge graph. note that the rules are monotone, as only the construct clause is allowed; in other words, triples can never be deleted or updated. regarding scalability with this type of rule, ref. [66] recently demonstrated that efficiency can be achieved by using the datalog and answer set programming reasoner dlv2 [67], which supports embedding sparql queries. based on the results in [66], the solution proposed in this paper appears then to be optimal both in terms of usability, as sparql is widely adopted both due to the rapid expansion of the semantic web and to its simple and intuitive syntax, and in terms of scalability, since the rules can be integrated into the dlv2 reasoner, which is highly efficient. given the highlighted shacl-sparqls peculiarities, this paper extends the research directions initiated in [2] by demonstrating how the regulations discussed in the previ- ous section can be represented using shacl-sparql. these norms involve temporal reasoning and aggregate functions, which are extremely challenging, if not impossible, to represent in the other rule-based formats mentioned earlier. to the best of our knowledge, no prior attempts have been made to model these norms in those formats. however, before presenting our implementation in shacl-sparql, it is necessary to understand how it is possible to represent time-stamped knowledge in rdf, i.e., knowledge that features a start and an end date, which are relevant to temporal reasoning. in this paper, as mentioned above, we follow the time-indexed value in context (tvc) ontology design pattern, which will be discussed in the next section. 5. time-indexed value in context (tvc) in our case study, time plays an important role in determining whether a participant in the upstream petroleum industry is compliant with li 2204. hence, for acc to be achieved in the area of local content and participation of ghanas upstream petroleum industry, the temporal aspects must be represented. for instance, an employee of a company can play different roles at different times within his or her employment period, as we discussed in section 2.1 above. information 2024, 15, 759 12 of 27 shacl-sparql does not suffice to deal with the complexities of time management. shacl-sparql is a language for querying and reasoning with rdf data, it is not con- cerned with how to represent knowledge in rdf, i.e., how to properly represent features and values of conceptual entities that vary over time. the time-indexed value in context (tvc) ontology design pattern has instead been proposed to this end. we chose to use it to represent our case study. tvc, described as an extension of the time-indexed situation pattern (http://www. ontologydesignpatterns.org/cp/owl/timeindexedsituation.owl, accessed 17 november 2024), is an owl ontology providing a comprehensive and flexible framework for describ- ing instances where entities have values (e.g., roles, statuses) within contexts and during specific time frames [68]. its classes and properties allow specifying values that concep- tual entities have in certain contexts that vary over time, enabling representation of time periods during which entities had particular values, as well as the contexts within which entities have those values, which is significant since values may depend on the considered context, e.g., an employee being a foreign employee in a subsidiary but indigenous in the parent company. the main classes and properties of the tvc ontology design pattern are shown in figure 1. the central class is valueintime class, which defines a specific type of temporally organized scenario that serves as a focal point connecting an entity with a specific value, encompassing the value itself, and considering the temporal and contextual boundaries that influence the relationship between the entity and the value (the tvc ontology design pattern figure from https://sparontologies.github.io/tvc/current/tvc.html, accessed 17 november 2024). figure 1. tvc ontology design pattern. tvc includes four main object properties. one of these, hasvalue, specifies in its range the class valueintime: this property is used to associate an individual of the state of affairs to a particular value in time and context. we will use this property to, for instance, associate the employees with their role; the latter, as explained above, is a value in time (i.e., it has a start and end date), in the context of a particular company. the other three object properties relate the value in time and context with the features describing it; therefore, the class valueintime is specified in their domain rather than in their range. these object properties are as follows:    attime, which defines the individual instant or interval in which the entity has a value. the classes instant or interval are part of the time ontology (https://www. entity. withincontext, which defines the relevant context in which an entity holds the value, e.g., the company in which the employee is employed. tvc makes it possible to represent all the employment details of an employee within different periods and contexts. through tvc, we can represent the employment period of a ghanaian employee, the various roles played by that employee within that period, which we chose for the use case of this paper. in order to implement acc within the local content and participation domain of the upstream petroleum sector of ghana, we encoded, in line with the recent literature (cf. section 4 above), the chosen regulations into shacl-sparql rules because of their expressivity and logical reasoning capabilities. in particular, section 4 above explained that the solution proposed in this paper is rather close to the solution proposed in [60]: both solutions are based on sparql, which make them capable of handling aggregate and temporal information. on the other hand, sparql alone is unable to prioritize the rules while shacl-sparql vocabulary includes the property sh:order, which allows us to specify the execution order of the rules, thus allowing for (some) control flow within the framework. specifically, sh:order is a datatype property which associates rules with integers, and rules are then executed from the lowest to the highest value specified in sh:order; in cases where two rules are associated with the same value, their order of execution is randomly selected by the automated reasoner. in essence, sh enables the implementation of procedural programs that use sparql rules, of which our proposed approach is no exception. to achieve acc in complex use cases, e.g., our own, certain rules must be executed before others; thus, sparql alone does not provide the required expressivity. for instance, in section 2.1, it was stated in effect that compliance checking can only be conducted if the petroleum commission has received the main compliance filing document, information 2024, 15, 759 17 of 27 which is the local content plan (lcp). thus, without the lcp, the commission cannot conduct the necessary compliance checks. as a result, companies are obligated to submit their lcp within a particular time window. hence, in implementing an acc framework for this domain, the first rules to be executed must check whether the lcp plan has been submitted; if so, it has to subsequently check whether it has been submitted within the allowed time window. if a company has already violated these requirements, no further compliance checks can be conducted. it would be rather complex, if not impossible, to implement these rules via a single sparql rule as proposed in [60]. thanks to sh:order, on the other hand, it is easily possible to decouple complex compliance checks into a sequence of simpler sparql rules. furthermore, in the proposed framework, rule prioritization can be utilized to im- plement conflict resolution through rule overriding, which is essential in legal reasoning. it is, in fact, quite common to find specific rules in legislation that override general rules in particular contexts. however, conflict resolution falls outside the scope of this paper, which focuses on aggregative and temporal reasoning. we refer interested readers to [2,66], which discuss shacl-sparql rules for managing rule overriding through priorities, fully compatible with the rules presented in this paper. 6.1. checking constraints including temporal constraints this subsection presents the formalization of the selected norms from our use case. however, before we proceed, it must be clarified that the goal of the developed production rule system is to identify companies that did not comply with the regulations, so they can be penalized in accordance with regulation 46 of li 2204 (regulation 46 of the petroleum (local content and local participation) regulations, 2013 (l.i 2204).). therefore, we designed the rules to identify companies that violate specific compliance checks, flagging them as non-compliant during the execution process. this is carried out by adding the companies within a special class noncompliantcompany while associating them with an explanation in plain text through the property rdfs:comment (see examples below). the rules are executed sequentially. the sequence ensures that a company is only subjected to a rule if it has not already been flagged as non-compliant by any of the preceding rules, thereby optimizing the overall process. the first rule, having sh:order equal to 0, searches for companies that did not submit their lcps at all. this rule goes through the provided abox of the ontology to identify all companies which have not submitted an lcp. it then flags all such companies as non-compliant. as specified supra, if a company is flagged as non-compliant, no further checks can be conducted on it. on the other hand, companies that pass the first check will be subsequently checked by the second rule, having sh:order equal to 1. given this value on the sh:order property, this rule will only be executed after the previous one. in order to understand whether the companies passed the first check, this rule will check whether they were not flagged as non-compliant. for all such companies, the second rule checks whether they submitted their lcps outside the time frame within which the lcps were meant to be submitted. the rule goes through the provided abox of the ontology to identify the actual submission dates of the lcps by these companies in order to check whether they fall within or outside the stipulated window of submission. as we have seen above in the shacl shapes (2) and (5), sparql, contrary to owl, allows for the comparison of xsd:datetime values. if the rule identifies any submission date that falls outside the window of submission, it again flags the company as non-compliant. it should now be clear to the reader how we decided to implement control flow on the different rules. they are executed in a sequence, by setting incremental values on the sh:order property. rules search for companies that violate the implemented checks, by flagging them as non-compliant as long as the rules are executed. rules in the information 2024, 15, 759 18 of 27 sequence are executed on a company content and participation in ghanas upstream petroleum industry (li 2204). in his phd research, the first author analyzes numerous provisions in li 2204 and other related legislation, which require industry participants to utilize indigenous ghanaian services, such as legal and financial services. these regulations were then translated into shacl-sparql rules. however, to maintain brevity and focus, this paper does not present all of them, but instead highlights selected rules that showcase two key features of using shacl-sparql for legal reasoning: aggregation and temporal reasoning. the high expressiveness of sparql allows for the modeling of complex regulations requiring aggregate functions and temporal management, while shacl enables defeasible reasoning through its priority operators. owl, on the other hand, is primarily designed for reasoning about relationships and hierarchies, rather than for quantitative operations like counting or calculating. moreover, owl lacks native constructs for temporal reasoning and defeasibility, both of which are crucial in legal reasoning. alternative approaches have therefore been proposed to extend rdf, owl, swrl, and other languages with new predicate symbols that introduce specific semantics, as shown in [69,70]. however, we believe that shacl-sparql rules, which are gaining traction within the semantic web community [9,64,65,71,72], provide an integrated language with the necessary expressiveness. since shacl-sparql integrates sparqlwidely used as the primary formal language for working with rdf datait could more easily lead to standardized solutions. these solutions may also involve domain experts not necessarily familiar with formal constraints. in other words, while approaches based on owl and other formal systems have been effective in their respective domains, shacl-sparqls usability and integration of sparql provide a unified framework for addressing various aspects that have historically been studied independently. as the present paper focuses on the expressivity needed to represent the norms of our use case, which might be provided by shacl-sparql but not by owl or other contempo- rary languages for legal reasoning, it might be argued that, on the other hand, the proposed solution may not be efficient and scalable. indeed, scalability and efficiency have always been core motivations in owl research, where many proposed solutions include mathe- matical proofs to show that computations remain within decidable boundaries to control computational complexity (see [54]). however, as discussed towards the end of section 4 above, recent publications involving the second author of this paper, in particular [2,66], demonstrate quite the opposite: shacl-sparql rules are significantly more efficient and scalable than most mainstream automated reasoners for the legal domain. specifically, the experiments in [2,66] show that the asp-based reasoner dlv2 [67], which can interface with and process sparql rules, achieves the best performance, even outperforming other asp-based reasoners. this paper represents the first attempt to apply the frameworks explored in [2,66], which have only been evaluated using synthetic datasets, to a real-world use case: specific regulations within ghanas upstream petroleum industry. despite the positive outcomes of the research presented here, significant challenges remain in developing an application capable of processing all data submitted to the ghana petroleum commission, including the local content plan, the local content performance report, and other required compliance filing documents. one such challenge arises from the fact that these documents require companies to include textual content explaining how they achieved compliance with certain legal requirements outlined in the regulations. the relevant data must then be automatically extracted from this content and formalized in rdf format, prior to processing via shacl-sparql. to achieve this, we plan to integrate large language models (llms) into the final application. we believe that combining llms with knowledge graphs is a highly promising yet challenging direction in ai research, and we aim to contribute to this overall effort in our future work. specifically, following the approach recently adopted in [19], which used information 2024, 15, 759 24 of 27 gpt-3 to classify gdpr provisions while fine-tuning the llm on the corpus from [24], we are now conducting similar experiments by fine-tuning llms on the manually crafted shacl-sparql rules
