classification of challenges in achieving bim-based safety-requirement checking in vertical construction projects poornachandra venkatesh1 and semiha ergan, a.m.asce2 abstract: due to the one-off nature of projects, which necessitates setting up of unique production sites, the construction industry requires stringent requirements for safety of the workforce and the public around job sites. although there are various federal (e.g., osha) and local safety regulations (e.g., nyc department of buildings codes) to keep construction sites safe and incident free, around 20% of work fatalities in the private industry have been from the construction industry in the past years. within the current practice, the requirements listed in these regulations are kept within document-based safety plans. the challenge is the large number of safety requirements to be checked with more than 1,000 sections and multiple narratives under each. given this, the manual approach fails in tracking whether these requirements are properly considered in safety plans, hence resulting in omissions and accidents/incidents due to inadequate implementations of safety requirements. although automated safety compliance-checking coupled with building information modeling (bim) has been an opportunity to bring efficiency to the process, there are several challenges that stand in the way of the ideal practice. this research provides the results of rigorous research conducted with the guidance of practitioners in the construction safety and insurance domain, and analysis and implementation of osha part 1926 narratives using model-based checking tools. the results have been consolidated and presented as a taxonomy of challenges along with potential solutions to these challenges. researchers and practitioners (software vendors, construction companies, regulators) can build on the outcomes to generate solutions to address these challenges. doi: 10.1061/jcemd4.coeng-13119.  2023 american society of civil engineers. author keywords: model-based safety; building information model (bim); safety regulations; automated inspection; occupational safety and health administration (osha); department of buildings (dob). introduction the dynamic nature of construction job sites exposes workers and the public to safety issues and makes the industry one of the most injury-prone industries. accidents and near misses are observed at job sites despite federal and local regulations. the construction in- dustry accounts for one in five work fatalities in the private industry (bls 2017), with more than 26,000 construction worker casualties in the us, 40% of which involved falls from height, and 30% of the falls were caused by inadequate, removed, or inappropriate use of fall protection equipment. despite existing efforts for preventing safety injuries and near-misses, there is still a need for rethinking the traditional practices on construction safety. within the current practice, there are various federal [e.g., occu- pational safety and health administration (osha)] and local safety regulations [e.g., nyc department of buildings (dob codes)] available to ensure the safety of workers and the public at/around construction sites (osha 1926; nyc dob 2014). these regulations provide requirements to be implemented at job sites for maintaining safe working environments. a major solutions to perform rule-based checking of safety require- ments (e.g., solibri 2021; zhang et al. 2013; kim et al. 2016). these tools and developed solutions rely on a prepared bim to in- clude (or checking of safety requirements, this research aims to identify challenges that hinder model-based safety compliance checking and provide a taxonomy of challenges for practitioners and researchers to consider when preparing models and rulesets for effective and automated safety compliance checking. these requirements have been identified by examining all sections of part 1926 of osha with the planning safety planning plays a crucial role in the construction life cycle to avoid severe consequences such as delay, injury, and even loss of lives. however, construction sites are usually addressed as being underresourced and underplanned in terms of safety planning (e.g., zhang and hu 2011; egan 1998). personal protective equip- ment (ppe) is usually used as the last line to protect the workers from an unavoidable hazard (irizarry et al. 2005). regulations in- dicate that other than educating and training workers and providing the ppe, companies need to have more stringent safety and health implementations at sites depending on the nature of construction activities. these regulations are available at federal (e.g., osha part 1926) and local levels (e.g., nyc dob chapter 33) for com- panies to comply with at sites. although the federal and local level regulations provide an ex- tensive set of narratives for safety compliance at sites, studies clearly show that the current practice of construction safety plan- ning is document-based and checked through ineffective manual ob- servations at sites (zhang et al. 2013; ku and mills 2010). safety plans are generated using 2d drawings manually, and then checked by inspectors if they are properly and proactively implemented at sites. this document and experience-based safety compliance check- ing is dependent on subjective interpretations and thus error-prone. another challenge is the voluminous nature of safety regulations, which makes it harder to track them for compliance. furthermore, construction site safety is mainly the responsibility of contractors; however, lack of proper thought process given during the design phase on the safety needs has been an ongoing problem to date (hinze and wiegand 1992; zhang et al. 2013; behm 2005). bims for construction safety virtual design and construction (vdc) and computational ap- proaches that reason with bims are widely used in the construction industry (e.g., egress analysis, clash detection, 4d, accessibility analysis) while the construction site safety still undergoes the manual and people-centric approaches. with the wider adoption of the technology, the use of bims has gained the grounds by allowing information exchange through computer-interpretable formats (eastman 2009; staub-french and khanzode 2007). gradually, bim provided the opportunity of bringing automation into safety compliance. previous studies to address safety concerns through bims are various and can be grouped into studies that leverage models for (1) visualization and interactive learning environments for safety training (e.g., li et al. 2018; vries et al. 2004); (2) hazard identi- fication (e.g., benjaoran and bhokha 2010; zhang et al. 2015); (3) proactive site safety and layout planning (e.g., sulankivi et al. 2009); and (4) safety compliance checking (e.g., zhang et al. 2013). various research approaches that leverage models and model- checking tools have been proposed in the literature for safety com- pliance checking. for example, zhang et al. (2013) presents an automated rule-based checking system by leveraging data stored in bims and how future safety planning can be integrated with the generation of work breakdown structures and project schedules. however, these studies rely on models that contain the objects (e.g., scaffolding), attributes (e.g., scaffolding top platform width), and object decompositions (e.g., scaffolding composed of guard- rails, toe boards, platforms) that are typically mentioned in safety narratives, and assume that models that contain such data are readily available to contractors. despite the potential provided by the bims in construction safety checking and management, models that contain safety related objects, attributes, and object decompo- sitions are not readily available, and there is still a need to charac- terize possible challenges in converting initial design models into models that are usable for safety compliance checking. hence, our work complements the previous research studies that leverage model data for safety compliance checking but significantly differs from them by presenting a missing-but-needed classification of challenges that prevent full deployment of model-based safety compliance checking. this research also provides potential solu- tions to practitioners and researchers to overcome challenges and prepare models for proactive compliance checking. research method the objectives of this research were to identify and classify the challenges inherent in the process of rule-based checking of safety requirements, and provide potential solutions for these challenges that practitioners and software vendors can consider during imple- mentation. the challenges have been investigated in the process with respect to the interpretation of narratives in regulations, rep- resentation of rulesets, and preparation of models for automated checking of them using models. fig. 1 shows the overview of the research method. the regulations analyzed in this study included osha part 1926- safety and health regulations for construction. this investigation included (1) screening sections in osha part 1926 to identify whether sections are possible to check with infor- mation that could be stored in bims; (2) for the sections that are highlighted, analyzing each narrative and labeling them for their potential to be converted to computer interpretable rulesets; (3) con- verting marked narratives to computer-interpretable rulesets, and recording information needed in bims for these ruleset instances; and (4) conducting experiments with model-checking tools to link identified safety requirements to testbed bims and recording chal- lenges observed while preparing models for rule checking. while defining the rulesets, preparing models for rule/model- based compliance checking, and linking models with the rulesets, all the challenges faced that hindered effective linkage of safety requirements to bims were documented throughout the process. while some of the narratives could not be represented as computer interpretable rules to check, if there is a model element that a safety narrative ties to, we marked them as safety notifications to be displayed regarding those elements. for example, for the form deck guard rails section, one narrative indicates, each employee on the face of formwork or reinforcing steel shall be in achieving model-based safety checking and potential solutions the results showed that if the rulesets are implemented in the model-checking tools, the tool can successfully check the viola- tions automatically and save tremendous amounts of time and hu- man efforts. however, along the way various types of challenges were identified during the process of the safety requirement iden- tification and ruleset development. these challenges are provided in table 3 with their definitions. identified challenges fall mainly under four categories: chal- lenges that are related to model content, nature of the narratives, model-checking tools, and construction. under model content, the challenges are mainly due to missing (i.e., components are not in the model), incomplete (i.e., component is there but required attribute is missing), or incorrect representations (i.e., whole-part relationships are not defined for components) in models. narrative specific challenges are due to (1) having narratives without a clear language that indicates thresholds/boundaries that should be imple- mented (i.e., vagueness in narratives); (2) having narratives with compounding requirements, where decomposition is needed for proper implementation; or (3) having narratives that are mainly pro- visional and cannot be linked to model components. tool related challenges can be faced while implementing model-based safety requirements checking, and these are mainly (1) due to inflexibility of tools in their library of logical operators required for properly implementing the osha 1926 narratives; and (2) due to lack of a mapping classification for representing objects that are uncom- mon in 3d models but needed in narratives through the use of common object types (where this classification will help to map these uncommon objects always to those common objects) to bring consistency to ruleset implementation. construction phasing is an- other challenge, where narratives refer to a specific phase where certain safety requirements be present on the site and the models need to be prepared to enable model-based checking. fig. 4 pro- vides the main contribution of this work, which is the classification of challenges in the way to model-based safety compliance check- ing. the details of these challenges and potential viable solutions to eliminate (or have a workaround to these challenges) are provided in the following subsections. model specific challenges: missing components in bims and potential solutions although design models are available to construction teams, and detailed analysis of narratives in osha part 1926 resulted in a categorized list of challenges in the way toward fully automated safety compliance checking. the aim is to enable proactive checking of the applicable rules when the next phase of the construction is about to start so any safety re- lated concerns could be flagged as violations for inspectors ahead of construction. the research team analyzed the federal safety re- quirements (osha part1926) within 29 subparts with respect to their potential for automated compliance checking. the analyzed narratives from osha 1926 were then converted to software im- plementable rulesets, and the rules were checked against the model using a bim validation tool. the research team identified four major groups of challenges on the way to automated safety checking: model, narrative, tool, and construction specific. model specific challenges are related to what bims provide as data and show that models lack component types that are typical in the safety domain but not part of standard object libraries, lack spe- cialized attributes that are referred in narratives, or lack whole/part representations that are quite referred in osha narratives but not practically represented in models. narrative specific challenges are observed with respect to the vagueness in the language to accurately convert them to rulesets, or the compound nature of narratives that necessitate multiple rulesets to be defined per nar- rative. model-checking tools also pose additional constraints on the way to automated safety compliance with their limitations on logical operator selections and modifications, as well as ways to maintain a standard object mapping terminology that can be utilized when component types referred in narratives are not in model authoring tools and are replaced with standard com- ponent types. lastly, construction phasing information is needed for grouping components that will be constructed in the next phase so that related rules can be checked for those components only. this work provides a taxonomy of challenges in the way to fully automated safety compliance checking using models based on de- tailed investigation and implementation of osha part1926 narra- tives under 20 subparts that can be represented in bims. this work also provides practitioners and researchers with potential solutions to these challenges so model-based checking can be fully imple- mented. the outcome is the first one that systematically provides a classification of these challenges. findings provide a foundation for researchers to provide solutions to eliminate such challenges. several practitioner groups can benefit from the outcomes of this work. construction teams would benefit from the outcomes by understanding the challenges and proactively devising alternative paths to solve them if they are adopting model-based compliance checking in their practice. model-checking and bim authoring tool vendors can improve their categories of object libraries, object decomposition representations, and representation of specialized attributes, and extend the ruleset representation operators and
