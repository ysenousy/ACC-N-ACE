proposed with the increasing interest and research of digital twins for construction. several large research projects in europe are ongoing (e.g., cogito [40] and bim2twin [41]), each developing an ontology to represent their captured knowledge and needs. while the different representations share the same objective (i.e., to capture the historical, planned, and future state of the construction situation to facilitate an improved con- struction industry), the approaches still vary slightly. some strategies invite unprocessed data, and some prefer information and knowledge (i. e., processing data elevates it to information and combinations if in- formation creates knowledge [23]). ontologies capture the relevant classes and relations that are specific enough to represent the data, in- formation, and knowledge but general enough for similar use cases. many ontologies exist for different purposes, and reuse of both ontol- ogies and schemas should be considered when creating new ones, as is the case with the digital twin construction ontology (dtco) [42], which relies on, e.g., building topology ontology [43] and building element ontology [44]. however, dtco does not represent safety- related classes and relationships. within the domain of safety in construction, previous work has been investigating ontologies to capture and represent safety-related classes and their relationships to other classes within and outside of the safety domain. among others, [4] presented an ontology that connected the potential hazards (i.e., the new class of the safety ontology) to building elements through job steps, activities, and tasks (i.e., classes of existing ontologies). the safety ontology also captured the potential hazards and their relation to mitigation and safety regulation. in [45], the safety ontology presented in [4] was extended with the capabilities to repre- sent potential hazards to define hazard spaces, represented as spatial artifacts. [34] extended and detailed the spatial artifacts to cover falls- from-heights and struck-by-falling objects hazards in 4d. while ontologies are available for capturing construction project information and others for safety information, they must be merged to exploit interdisciplinary knowledge. this also means that additional classes and relations are needed to connect the domains. thus, this study will reuse and extend the existing ontologies to incorporate these con- nections and additional safety-related perspectives. there is a growing body of research that investigates the integration of knowledge graphs, ontologies and llms [46,47]. key areas of inte- gration include using knowledge graphs as a form of structure training data [48], and using llms for constructing knowledge graphs [49]. towards exploiting such an integration in the construction safety domain, the research presented in this paper focuses on establishing an conceptually-rich ontology and data model of construction safety that can complement the growing role of llms in construction site analysis. 2.2. digital twins and related technologies digital twins represent a significant advancement in the construction industry, offering real-time monitoring and integration capabilities that surpass traditional methods such as 4d bim and sparql-based query engines. while 4d bim integrates time-related information with 3d models to enhance project scheduling and visualization, digital twins extend this by creating dynamic, real-time representations of physical assets. this allows for continuous monitoring and optimization throughout the project lifecycle [10]. 4d bim, which combines 3d models with the time dimension, has been instrumental in improving project planning and visualization. however, it lacks the real-time data integration and dynamic updating capabilities of digital twins [9]. sparql-based query engines are effective for querying structured data but do not support the dynamic, real-time updates required for construction safety management [50]. the proposed framework leverages the strengths of digital twins to enhance safety planning by integrating real-time data and enabling more effective cross-domain knowledge exploitation. this integration allows for the continuous updating of safety information, providing decision-makers with timely and actionable insights. recent studies have highlighted the transformative potential of digital twins in con- struction management, emphasizing their ability to enhance real-time monitoring, improve stakeholder collaboration, and optimise project planning and execution [51,52]. by combining digital twins with knowledge graphs, our approach uniquely enables the seamless integration of safety and planning data, facilitating more informed decision-making and improving overall project safety and efficiency. 2.3. technologies for improved safety in construction as it is described in dtcs [53], safety should be considered con- cerning four main responsibilities: 1) planning (prevention through design and planning), 2)inspection (conformance checking), 3) right- time identification and mitigation of arising hazards in the production (proactively), and 4) improving work crews safety performance (training), which all require data and creates information that can improve the safety situation in construction. different strategies have been proposed in the planning domain to automate identifying and mitigating construction hazards in 3d bim models, e.g., [2,5,54]. some studies extend the functionality to consider the schedule in combination with the 3d bim model (i.e., 4d bim or digital twins, where geometry and schedule are also represented) [4,34,55] or semantic analysis, e.g., [7,56,57]. recent research high- lights the transformative potential of digital twins in construction management, emphasizing their ability to enhance real-time moni- toring, improve stakeholder collaboration, and optimize project plan- ning and execution [51,52]. for instance, digital twins have been shown to increase the transparency of information and provide real-time feedback, which is critical for effective safety management [51]. how- ever, the connection between safety, construction elements, and schedules still must be captured. spreadsheet-based checklists (e.g. via excel) are an effective tool in construction safety engineering owing to their simplicity and familiarity by many users [5860]. the main advantage of knowledge graphs over only generating checklists is enhanced comprehensibility through vi- sualizations, semantic richness, verifiability and automation: such checklists can be automatically generated if one has access to a knowl- edge graph; 3d visualizations and 2d diagrams can be automatically generated from knowledge graph linked to a bim model; knowledge graphs can be formally verified (i.e. assessed in terms of geometry, soundness and completeness, and so on). 3d models can be complex and challenging to assess upfront. therefore, manual procedures often only perform short-term look- aheads, which means information about the total amount of safety material and where to apply it is rarely available [22]. furthermore, 4d models can be represented as a sequence of evolving 3d models, which increases the complexity and, therefore, the time consumption for manual approaches. the complexity of the problem is also rising for automated systems, but the impact of necessary manual labor input and costs related to safety planning is constant. however, the complexity of interacting with the results of automated safety planning is increasing when the planning is performed for longer durations and for additional hazard types.. even though efforts in safety planning are becoming increasingly accurate, the reality might deviate from the plan. there are several reasons for temporarily removing the mitigation measures to access workspaces, and sometimes, these are forgotten to be replaced. equip- ment can also be worn out due to the rough environment or even incompliant installations from the beginning. the safety-responsible person(s) must traverse the job site frequently to ensure continued 3 k.w. johansen et al. advanced engineering informatics 65 (2025) 103305 compliance, which is labor-intensive, especially when large sites have high activity in several locations and issues gradually evolve. re- searchers investigated different strategies to support compliance checking (a.k.a safety inspection), including computer vision from different perspectives [3] and point clouds in combination with deep learning [61]. however, these methods all produce information about compliance but cannot fix the issues themselves. therefore, humans must be brought into the loop, and the identified compliance issues must be presented clearly and concisely to the practitioners to avoid over- whelming them. while the details of bim models and schedules are improving, it is still impossible to foresee all hazard incidents solely based on these. therefore, identification and mitigation of safety incidents, besides compliance issues, during the execution of the construction project are necessary. those cases are referred to as dynamic incidents, e.g., over- head crane swings or pedestrian workers not using the designated paths, machine-to-machine- and pedestrian-to-machine-incidents. identifying these kinds of incidents requires additional sensor techniques to record where the entities are and how they interact. [6267] are all examples where location tracking technologies are used to identify dynamic hazards, and some, in combination with the cloud, enable them to operate in digital twin settings. similarly, these incidents have to be presented to the stakeholders to increase awareness and improve safety behavior. however, presenting long lists of dynamic safety incidents to the practitioners is overwhelming and does not allow them to act easily to improve the situation. besides acting directly on the construction site, the safety insights about hazards and increased model details facilitate the creation of realistic training scenarios for the workers in virtual reality (vr). the virtual training allows the workers to be trained in environments rep- resenting the concrete construction site at the concrete state where the current progress is considered (i.e., as built) [6870]. the training im- pacts the workers behavior and awareness, and the training perfor- mance can be used in the planning when the crew allocation is decided. the many technology-driven approaches to improve the construc- tion safety situation all produce information that needs to be assessed by human stakeholders. however, if this information is not provided in format that is easy to understand and exploitable then it ends up over- whelming practitioners, resulting in critical safety information being lost. additionally, there are cases where information produced from individual tools can be even more useful if it is combined with other pieces of information and delivered to stakeholders as knowledge. as the safety planning domain is the most developed and streamlined compared to other domains, such as safety inspection and safety behavior monitoring, this work focuses on capturing and representing that information. 2.4. construction scheduling construction scheduling plays a vital role in successfully executing construction projects. the main objective of scheduling is to estimate the cost and time of completion and organize the workflow to fulfill de- pendencies [71]. planning a complete construction project is a highly time-consuming task, which motivates the efforts of automated con- struction scheduling and planning. these automated approaches include case-based reasoning [72], knowledge-based [73], model-based [74], constraint-based [75], genetic algorithms [76], expert systems [77], neural networks [78], and other methods [79]. the decision-makers must select the most desirable one from a selection of automatically generated schedules based on some key performance indicators (kpis), such as duration, cost, and planning risk assessment of critical path [80]. however, automated construction scheduling that considers lean prin- ciples and incorporates automated safety planning is still unavailable for decision-makers when selecting the desired construction schedule. therefore, it has yet to be possible to identify the impact that a changed construction task sequence will have on the safety situation on site. while the construction planning domain has been investigated to assist practitioners in planning procedures, there is still not a lot of focus on integrating safety planning into the broader construction planning domain. one reason is the lack of maturity of automated safety analysis and the lack of support for stakeholders in their interaction with safety analysis results. the lack of maturity is evident as the tools are not yet widely implemented in current practices due to a lack of trust, lack of fit to current workflows[81], lack of support by regulation, and lack of organizational commitment [82]. additionally, there is a lack of stan- dardization in the automated approaches, which also covers the stan- dardization of how to capture the safety analysis results, meaning that it is hard for the construction planning community to incorporate the safety information in their approaches [81]. this work also attempts to define the integration of safety analysis results in construction plans. 3. methodology while the domains of construction safety and construction planning are pushed forward individually in academia, they remain disconnected, as also pointed out in the previous section. this is also the case for the solutions that are already adopted and applied in the industry as pointed out in the introduction. therefore, this work investigates how the knowledge within an automatically created safety plan can benefit other domains of construction management, such as construction planning. fig. 2 illustrates the proposed framework, capturing the steps that will be performed and their outcome. the remainder of this document will follow the structure presented in this figure. the first step is to identify domain-relevant questions to ensure that the solution is relevant to the industry and the practitioners. the ques- tions have been identified through interactions with practitioners on construction sites in germany, spain, and finland, which ensures a wide perspective within europe. the practitioners are domain experts in construction management with years of experience working on different aspects of construction management. subsequently, these questions are sorted based on query category and the current availability of infor- mation in the safety plan (i.e., the information that is extractable with the safety analysis tool used). the query categories (i.e., entity, exis- tential, relationship, exploratory, attribute, comparative, list, count search (further explained in table 3)) are used to determine the nature of the query, which can identify the expected result type. two things must be in place before the query engine can be used to answer the questions. first, the natural-language-formulated questions need to be transformed into queries that a query engine can interpret using the query building blocks (table 3). the query building blocks can ease the later transformation in this study, but also in the longer term, where others have to extend the solution. the other is to define an ontology to capture the bim, schedule, and safety analysis result infor- mation in a knowledge graph that can be queried for extraction of domain knowledge. the two tasks are interdependent as the queries must be formed such that they are using the proposed ontology. it has been chosen to capture the knowledge graph in the ontology that was described [42], which captures the construction elements, processes, and tasks. however, the following section 4.2. after defining the ontology and queries, a knowledge graph needs to be populated for validation. for this study, it has been decided to cap- ture the graph in a local database tailored for domain-specialised knowledge representa- tion and reasoning, which aligns well with our aim to capture construction safety knowledge, and to make safety analysis more readily accessible to stakeholders, which we accomplish via prologs native querying engine. moreover, prolog is a suitably powerful programming 4 k.w. johansen et al. advanced engineering informatics 65 (2025) 103305 this is also why it is among the most popular declarative languages in research and commercial prod- ucts. its query engine allows for fast interaction with a domain captured as facts, which allows for fast lookups and recursions that allow tracing through (logical) relations. other options for representing knowledge graphs exist (e.g., neo4j [85], graphdb [86], or other declarative op- tions the individual domains (i.e., rq 2) and cross- domain (i.e., rq 3). the first part ensures better exploitation within the safety domain, and the second part ensures that the solution can also move the boundaries in the more advanced cross-domain impact esti- mation and optimization situation. even though the questions in the safety domain consider the effect of the schedule (safety analysis has been based on 4d bim), the answers do not consider changes and, thus, become specifically safety-related. on the other hand, the cross-domain questions consider the impact of safety changes on the construction plan and, vice versa, the impact of the construction plan changes on the safety situation. the following questions (q1 to q7 in italics) have been identified in situations where the practitioners have been presented with safety planning of a scheduled construction situation, captured in 4d bim. thus, the safety analysis is performed automatically each time the construction site was planned to change (i.e., completion of construction tasks). additionally, the questions have been inspired by the reasons that have been identified to be the source of real incidents, such as the ac- cident in ludwigshafen, germany [95], and in hamburg, germany [96], which in both cases involved poor planning of hazard mitigation and synchronization between stakeholders. the questions will represent domain-specific variables with # followed by a letter (e.g., #x or #y), and their rationale is presented in the following: q1: which hazards are work crew #x exposed to while they complete task #y?. knowing which kinds of hazards have been identified to expose a crew during the task makes them more aware of what to pay extra attention to, and in some cases, it is even worthwhile for them to learn or refresh their mind on how to act in these situations. eventually, this learning can be performed in a vr environment as proposed in [6870], where the selected training scenarios are based on the specific hazards exposing the crew during their task(s). q2: are hard hats required while performing task #x?. similarly, the stakeholders are interested in particular hazard types, e.g., if the crew is exposed to struck-by falling object hazards and must wear hard hats while performing a certain task. current practice on the construction site is to make a site-level requirement of wearing the hard hat, which in some cases is not reasonable. still, the only option for safety planning with the manual approaches does not practically allow safety planning in high enough resolution to separate specific areas. this leads to safety fatigue, and the workers think there is no reason to comply with the requirements, even though the dangers are luring around the site. this situation can be avoided if the workers were given a concrete reason to wear hard hats, and the requirement can be lifted when there was no reason to uphold it. however, it should be mentioned that automated safety tools at this stage still need further testing and improvements of scheduled activities (i.e., only planned tasks can be automatically safely assessed). for this reason, the tools should only be considered as assisting measures. 5 k.w. johansen et al. advanced engineering informatics 65 (2025) 103305 q3: are any guard rails installed for shorter than #x number of days?. there are situations where the falls-from-height hazards appear for only a short duration of time, e.g., when a slab element has been placed and the wall elements are placed immediately after. for these cases, it does not make sense to install guardrails, especially if there are no other tasks planned in the area between the slab installation and wall instal- lation, i.e., the only crew that would be presently exposed to the fall hazards would be the crew removing the guardrails to install the wall. in this case, it is beneficial to identify if the fall hazard remains longer than a practical duration and if any other crews are planned in that area in the meantime. if that is the case, it is more profitable and safe to fence off access to the slab and instruct the wall installation crew to use harnesses instead of installing and removing guardrails. this is also part of com- mon practices nowadays, but this work enables the construction man- ager to list all the situations and retrieve formal arguments for the verdict. additionally, it allows the construction manager to gain further insight into the impact of different durations. q4: how is the overall safety situation be affected if crew #x is swapped for crew #y?. if historical information about the construction crews safety per- formance is available, it can be used to improve the crew assignment and obtain improved safety. the information can be collected based on a combination of safety training performance and records of loss of con- trol events [55]. with this information available, it is possible to identify cases where swapping two crews could lead to a lower overall hazard exposure for the construction workers. additionally, it will have a more significant impact if the scheduled tasks can be swapped, but that would require capturing scheduling dependencies in the knowledge graph to know the available options and that the planning is performed itera- tively between general planning and safety analysis. this way, sched- uling constraints can remain fulfilled while the wiggle room is exploited to improve the safety of the construction site. q5: how much guardrail must be installed and removed tomorrow?. foreseeing how much hazard mitigation equipment to install and remove the following day, in three days, or during the next week enables the safety management to plan the workforce needed to install it. additionally, this knowledge can be used by the planning team to consider the local transportation of the equipment. moving the equip- ment closer to the application point must be planned in the crane schedule to ensure optimal utilization and avoid disturbance of the other planned crane activities. therefore, it is useful from a site logistic perspective and safety perspective to ensure that the required amount of guardrailing is available on the site and in the right area of the con- struction site or building story in the building when needed. q6: how much guardrail is needed to keep the site safe?. in the initial planning stage, the safety management must ensure that the necessary mitigation equipment is available to keep a site compliant with the regulation. extracting the amount directly from the safety plan eliminates the need to estimate and guess the amount, affecting the sites storage and continued availability. capturing the number is, however, more complex as there might be varying reuse policies based on the equipment location and temporal overlap (i.e., different combinations of the installed and removed amount). q7: how is the project be affected if delivery truck #x is postponed by one day?. in a situation where the construction management is informed about a delivery truck being delayed for an unforeseen reason and needs to quickly know how this affects the construction project regarding plan- ned safety. it depends on the truckload, but based on a list of delayed building elements, it can be identified if and which hazard spaces and mitigation equipment the elements would replace and the crews to inform about the delay. the crews must be informed that those pieces of safety mitigation equipment are to be kept even though they may appear in their plan to be removed. consequently, it improves the ability to plan day-to-day activities more agile and adapt to unforeseeable situations that are part of the nature of
